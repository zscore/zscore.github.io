sunflower-composition

Four animated pieces, each one a single C file that writes raw RGB frames to stdout. No libraries, no assets, no scene files — every pixel is computed from the code, piped straight into ffmpeg.

All four draw the same procedurally-grown sunflower from flower.h and differ only in what tells the flowers what to do. The videos below are compressed previews; the full renders and the long write-up are in the repo.

Pieces

4

  • sunflowers

    36×64 grid · 1440×2560 · 30 s

    The original. A cat is rasterised off-screen and its brightness tells each flower how far to open, so the field draws the cat as a halftone — a cat that peeks in from off-frame and the whole crop has to reorganise to render.

    The flowers never snap to their target. Each runs its own spring-damper integrator, so petals overshoot when the brightness arrives and settle afterwards. The field leads; the flowers lag, each by its own amount.

  • interference

    27×48 grid · 1080×1920 · 48 s · 12 movements

    The cat is thrown away and replaced by a single scalar function, always a superposition of wave terms, sampled once per frame at the centre of every one of the 1296 cells:

    V(c, r, t)  →  roughly [-1, 1]

    Three readings come off it. How far each flower opens is a smoothstep window on V — applied to |V| on the movements with standing nodes, which pins the nodal lines shut so you can see them as seams of closed buds. Which way each flower looks is tilt·∇V + curl·perp(∇V), so a head leans down the slope of the wave it is standing on. And its colour is hue += 9.5·V — crests run lemon-bright, troughs drop toward deep honey.

    Twelve movements of 4 s, cross-fading over the last 0.95 s. Every frequency is an integer harmonic of the 48 s loop, so the piece is exactly periodic: frame 1439 differs from frame 0 by about as much as any two ordinary consecutive frames.

  • lattice

    36×64 grid · 1080×1920 · 48 s · 8 movements

    interference rebuilt under one extra rule: no flower may ever move quickly. Instrumenting the older piece showed heads yawing at 5400°/s — not a wave effect but a bug of representation, since facing was integrated as a vector and a head sitting on a stationary point of the field could have its atan2 swing through 180° in one frame while nothing about it actually moved. Here facing is carried as (yaw, pitch) and integrated along the shortest arc, which removes that by construction. Yaw is capped at 46°/s.

    Slow turned out to be a design principle rather than a tax. What makes a field slow is not low spatial frequency but that its fine structure is stationary while only a coarse envelope evolves — and for a sum of plane waves whose time-dependence lives only in their phases, the rate of change is bounded independently of the wavenumbers. So the quasicrystal movement can be made as fine as 36 columns will sample for free. That mode has a name: it is the phason of a quasicrystal, which rearranges the tiling locally without sliding it anywhere. Structure that rearranges without travelling is exactly the kind of motion a field of flowers can actually perform.

  • heliotrope

    18×32 grid · 1080×1920 · 72 s · 6 movements

    The other pieces use the flowers as a display: a field is computed and the heads are the pixels it gets drawn with. This one runs on the coarsest grid in the repo, where one head is 120 px across, and at that size a head is too big to be a pixel. So it gets driven by two things at once — a sun it actually looks at, and the field coupled in as a signed side-scalar.

    Every petal also moves on its own. Each blade swings, lifts, droops and stretches on its own pair of harmonics and its own two phases, hashed from the cell and the petal index, sharing nothing with the blade beside it. That is the whole difference between a badge being animated and a head: petals that all move together read as a sprite being rotated no matter how slowly you rotate it, because rigid motion is the one thing a viewer recognises instantly.

    The amplitudes are deliberately tiny — the worst case in the piece is a petal tip moving 3.4 px/s, about one petal-width every six seconds. You are not meant to catch it happening; you are meant to look away and look back.

The flower

flower.h draws the head as a small three-dimensional object rather than a flat badge, and every piece includes it. One world-space sun lights the whole field: each head builds an orthonormal frame from its own yaw and pitch, and every ray floret, seed and bract is shaded through it, so the field agrees about where the light comes from. That is most of what separates it from a halftone of yellow dots.

The petals are surfaces, cupped across their width and drooped along their length, with no dark keyline anywhere — what separates one petal from the next is the shadow each lays along the edges of the one beneath, which is what a photograph shows and an inked outline does not. The disc is a baked 192² texture of a 200-floret phyllotaxis packing, so the parastichy spirals fall out of the packing rather than being drawn, and it runs about 45% of the head across — roughly what Helianthus does, and most of what keeps these from reading as gerberas.

Per-flower identity does the rest: petal count, spin, curl, size, one of three colourways, an individual openness gamma, and about one ray floret in twenty-two simply missing — all hashed from the cell's coordinates and fixed for the whole piece.
